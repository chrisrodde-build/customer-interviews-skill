## Why these four

The brief was customer interviews for marketing in B2B tech. Several excellent practitioners work mainly in product or UX research; these four were chosen because together they cover the whole marketing use: who to talk to and how to turn it into messaging (Laudi & Suellentrop), how to get reliable stories (Torres), how to avoid polite lies (Fitzpatrick), and how to interview buyers about competitive decisions at scale (Clozd).

Honorable mentions worth knowing: Steve Portigal (*Interviewing Users*) for interview craft; Joanna Wiebe (Copyhackers) for voice-of-customer copy mining; Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek, covered as the analysis lens below.

## Georgiana Laudi & Claire Suellentrop

**Who:** co-founders of Forget the Funnel (the consultancy now operates as Customer Led) and co-authors of *Forget the Funnel: A Customer-Led Approach for Driving Predictable, Recurring Revenue* (2023). Suellentrop previously led marketing at Calendly and brings a journalism and radio interviewing background. Their method has been used by SaaS companies including Wistia, SparkToro and Appcues.

**Core idea:** the Customer-Led Growth framework — get inside the heads of your best customers, map and measure their experience, then find the biggest growth levers. Interviews are framed explicitly as a marketing tool: reverse-engineer how your best customers found, chose and succeeded with you, so you can find more like them.

**What it contributes here**
- **Best customers, not all customers.** Criteria: happily paying, little hand-holding needed, urgent problem, retention or expansion potential, easy to market to.
- **Survey vs. interviews.** With roughly 500+ ideal customers, start with a survey; fewer, or one method only, choose interviews.
- **Saturation.** 10–12 interviews surface the pattern.
- **JTBD interview structure for B2B.** Life before the product, the trigger, the evaluation, onboarding, value now — and a published template of about 25 questions plus an invite email script.
- **Synthesis into messaging.** Quotes and themes for struggle, motivation and desired outcome; placed on an experience map (struggle, evaluation, growth phases) with customer milestones; turned into a messaging guide.

**Limitation to know:** focused on SaaS and recurring revenue; best-customer sampling deliberately excludes the people who didn't buy, so pair it with win-loss or non-switcher interviews when the decision is about who you're losing.

## Teresa Torres

**Who:** product discovery coach, author of *Continuous Discovery Habits*, runs Product Talk and the Story-Based Customer Interviews course (which includes B2B example interviews and an AI interview coach).

**Core idea:** continuous discovery — weekly customer touchpoints — with interviews that collect specific stories rather than asking direct questions.

**What it contributes here**
- **Research questions vs. interview questions.** What you want to learn is not what you ask. Direct questions produce fast, plausible, unreliable answers; story prompts ("tell me about the last time…") produce actual behavior.
- **Scoping.** Match the breadth of the story prompt to the outcome you're working on.
- **The golden rule.** Let the participant talk about what they care about most; don't force a story they don't have.
- **The interview snapshot.** A one-page synthesis of each interview — memorable quote, quick facts, insights, opportunities, experience map, and a photo — done one interview at a time.

**Limitation to know:** built for product teams discovering opportunities, not for competitive or messaging decisions; adapt the snapshot's "opportunities" to marketing needs.

## Rob Fitzpatrick

**Who:** entrepreneur and author of *The Mom Test* (2013; re-released in a new edition), widely used in startup accelerators and university entrepreneurship courses.

**Core idea:** ask questions so good that even someone who loves you can't lie to you. Three rules: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specifics in the past, not generics or opinions about the future; talk less and listen more.

**What it contributes here**
- **The three kinds of bad data:** compliments, fluff (generics, futures, hypotheticals), and ideas — plus what to do with each: deflect, anchor, dig.
- **Commitment and advancement:** a good conversation ends with a concrete next step, not "thanks, that was helpful." For marketing research: a referral, a reference, permission to quote.
- **A fast check for any question.** The basis of the Mom Test check in the guide reference and several rows of Rubrics B and C.

**Limitation to know:** written for founders validating new ideas; its "keep it casual, don't call it an interview" advice suits early discovery more than structured studies with existing customers, where clear consent and a recorded call are the norm.

## Clozd

**Who:** a win-loss analysis company that runs buyer interviews for B2B companies, publishes the Win-Loss 101 course and a widely cited question framework (Andrew Peterson), and says it has conducted tens of thousands of buyer interviews.

**Core idea:** win-loss interviews are probing, adaptive conversations with buyers soon after a decision — not scripted surveys.

**What it contributes here**
- **Five principles for question design:** start broad and let the buyer tell the story; cover core topics in every interview; prepare drill-downs for recurring themes; avoid closed-ended and scale questions (mostly *what* and *why*); adapt on the fly.
- **Core topics:** requirements, product impressions, sales experience, pricing and packaging, competitive landscape, buying committee, implementation expectations, decision timeline.
- **Timing and length:** reach out within about two weeks of the decision; keep it to 30 minutes or less.
- **Interviews vs. surveys:** if it needs a rating, it belongs in a survey; if it needs a story, it belongs in an interview. The best programs mix both.
- **Practice first:** run a couple of practice interviews internally before talking to a real buyer.
- **Neutral interviewers:** buyers are more candid with a third party than with the rep.

**Limitation to know:** Clozd sells services and software, so its guidance naturally emphasizes scale and third-party interviewers; the principles hold for a team doing a handful in-house.

## The lens: Bob Moesta's Four Forces

Push, pull, anxiety and habit — a switch happens only when push plus pull outweighs anxiety plus habit. Moesta's switch-interview timeline (first thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, first use) underlies the best-customer guide here. It's documented fully in the `positioning-and-messaging` skill; this skill uses it as the coding frame in Rubric D.

## Where they agree

- **Past behavior beats opinion.** All four.
- **Stories, not surveys, for the why.** Torres, Clozd, Laudi & Suellentrop.
- **Don't pitch.** Fitzpatrick most loudly; all four.
- **Adapt to the participant.** Torres's golden rule; Clozd's fifth principle.
- **Talk to the right people.** Laudi's best customers; Clozd's closest-to-the-decision buyer; Torres's recruiting discipline.

## Where they differ

- **Cadence.** Torres: continuously, weekly. Laudi & Suellentrop: a focused study of 10–12 per segment. Clozd: a standing program across the pipeline. This skill treats them as modes: a study for a decision, a program for ongoing intelligence.
- **Structure.** Clozd wants core topics covered in every interview for comparability; Torres keeps the guide to a story prompt and lets the story lead. This skill uses the story first and must-cover topics only where the story didn't reach.
- **Formality.** Fitzpatrick favors casual conversations that don't feel like interviews; Clozd and Laudi run scheduled, recorded, consented calls. For existing B2B customers, this skill follows the formal approach.
- **JTBD vocabulary.** Laudi & Suellentrop and Moesta use it; Torres frames things as opportunities; Clozd as decision drivers. They describe overlapping things with different words — don't blend the vocabularies in one deliverable.
