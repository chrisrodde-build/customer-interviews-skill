## Synthesize one at a time

Torres's discipline: synthesize each interview individually, as soon after it as possible. A snapshot written the same day beats a perfect analysis three weeks later, because memory of tone, hesitation and emphasis fades fast. Batch synthesis at the end of a study also makes the last few interviews loom largest.

Order after each interview:
1. Score it (Rubric C) — so you know how much to trust it
2. Write the snapshot — the human summary
3. Code it (Rubric D) — the structured row

## The interview snapshot

Torres's one-page template for a single interview has six parts. Adapted for marketing research:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ [Identifier — role, org type, segment]   Date · Interviewer│
│ "Memorable quote — the line that captures this person"   │
├─────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┤
│ Quick facts          │ Insights                          │
│ - Segment, size      │ - Surprising or notable things    │
│ - Prior approach     │   that aren't opportunities      │
│ - Alternatives       │                                   │
│ - Type & evidence wt │                                   │
├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┤
│ Opportunities        │ Experience map                    │
│ - Needs, pains,      │ Trigger → looking → deciding →    │
│   desires, in their  │ first use → value now             │
│   words              │ (or the churn/loss equivalent)    │
└─────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┘
```

Torres uses a photo to make the person memorable; for B2B marketing research, a short persona line (role, org type, situation) often does the same job and avoids storing images.

## The coding sheet

One row per interview, columns from Rubric D. Keep it in a shared sheet so everyone sees the same evidence. Add filter columns for segment, type and evidence weight.

## Finding patterns

### Themes (Laudi & Suellentrop)

Forget the Funnel synthesizes interviews into three columns, each with quotes and a theme that groups them:

| Struggle | Motivation | Desired outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Quote → theme | Quote → theme | Quote → theme |

Cluster quotes that say the same thing in different words, name the theme in customer language, and count how many interviews contribute. The struggle themes feed the problem statement; the desired outcomes feed value propositions and success milestones.

They then place the insights onto a customer experience map — struggle, evaluation, and growth phases — and identify the milestones a customer must reach to get value. For marketing, that map shows where messaging, onboarding and retention efforts should land.

### Forces by segment (Moesta)

Average force strength (Rubric D) per segment. Where the forces differ materially between groups, you've found a real segment. Where they don't, the split you assumed isn't one.

### Decision drivers (Clozd)

For win-loss, tally the top decision driver per interview by outcome:

| Driver | Wins citing | Losses citing | Most-named competitor |
|---|---|---|---|

Look for drivers that appear in losses but not wins — those are where the deal was decided. Compare buyer-stated reasons with CRM loss reasons; the gap is itself a finding.

### What to look for in every study
- **The real alternative.** Usually not the competitor leadership expects.
- **The trigger.** What turned annoyance into action. It's the moment marketing should meet.
- **The real channel.** How they first heard — often a peer or consultant.
- **The customer's category word.** What they'd type into a search box.
- **The anxiety that nearly stopped them.** Objection-handling material.
- **Surprises.** Anything that contradicts current belief is worth more than confirmation.

## The evidence standard

- **Report frequency honestly.** "7 of 11 best customers named a spreadsheet as their prior approach." Never "most customers" without a count.
- **Separate observed from inferred.** Findings say what was heard; implications say what it might mean. Label them.
- **Weight by quality.** A finding needs at least three interviews in the segment, at least two strong on Rubric C.
- **Quote with clearance.** Role and organization type by default; names only with permission.
- **State what it rules out.** The most useful line in many reports: "No interviewee cited price as a reason for leaving."
- **Note the sample's limits.** Who wasn't interviewed, and how that might bias the result.

## Hand-off formats

### For messaging and positioning (feeds `positioning-and-messaging`, Stage 0)

```
# Demand brief — [date, N interviews, segments]

## The struggling moment
One paragraph in customer language.

## Four Forces
Push / Pull / Anxiety / Habit — ranked, with verbatims and counts

## Language inventory
Their words for the problem, the category, the outcome; search phrases
Words only we use (candidates for retirement)

## Segments
Where forces differ materially, by segment

## What this rules out
Claims the evidence does not support

## Evidence quality
Interviews by evidence weight; gaps in the sample
```

### For win-loss

```
# Win-loss findings — [period, N wins, N losses]

## Headline
The one thing most likely to change win rate

## Decision drivers
Table by outcome, with verbatims

## Competitor patterns
Who we lose to, on what, in which segment

## Sales-process findings
What buyers said about the experience

## Pricing perception
How price was received and compared

## Recommendations
Owner and action per finding
```

### For churn

```
# Churn findings — [period, N interviews, segments]

## Reasons by segment
Ranked, with force strength and verbatims

## Where they went
Competitor, spreadsheet, nothing

## Moments of no return
When the decision became inevitable — and what, if anything, could have intervened

## Within our control / outside it
Split the reasons honestly

## Recommendations
```

Every report ends by answering the decision sentence from the research brief.

## Common mistakes

- **Batch synthesis three weeks later.** The nuance is gone.
- **Counting mentions instead of interviews.** One person saying "price" five times is one interview.
- **Paraphrased "quotes."** If it's in quotation marks, it's verbatim.
- **Themes named in house language.** Name them in the customer's words.
- **Confirmation hunting.** Look hardest for evidence against the current belief.
- **No answer to the decision.** A themes deck that changes nothing.
