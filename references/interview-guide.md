## Research questions vs. interview questions

Torres's central point: teams conflate what they want to learn with what they ask. Asked directly, people answer instantly, and the fast answer feels true but often isn't — it's filtered through how they'd like to think they behave. Not lying; just unreliable.

So for each research question, ask: *what specific story would answer this?* Then spend the interview collecting that story and listening for the answers inside it.

| Research question (never asked) | Story prompt (asked) |
|---|---|
| What triggers organizations to look for a new donor database? | "Take me back to when you first thought you needed something different. What was going on?" |
| How do buyers compare us with Competitor X? | "Walk me through how you narrowed down your options." |
| What do customers value most now? | "Tell me about the last time you used [product] for [job]. Start from what you were trying to get done." |
| Why do small accounts cancel in year one? | "Tell me about the moment you decided to cancel. What happened that week?" |

**Scope the prompt to the decision.** Torres's example: a team looking for brand-new markets asks about the last time someone did the broad activity; a team working on one part of the product scopes the prompt to that part. Too broad and you never reach the moment you care about; too narrow and you miss the context.

## The anatomy of a guide

```
1. Consent and context (2 min)
   Recording, purpose, how quotes are used, "no wrong answers,
   we're not here to sell."

2. Warm-up (2–3 min)
   Their role, their organization, who they work with on [job].
   Keep it short — it's rapport, not data.

3. Story prompt (the core)
   One or two prompts. Then mostly probes.

4. Timeline probes, by phase
   Pull specifics: when, who, what they did, what they used,
   what they compared, what almost stopped them.

5. Must-cover topics
   Only what must be comparable across interviews (competitors,
   pricing reaction, committee). Ask only if the story didn't
   already cover it.

6. Close (3 min)
   "What should I have asked that I didn't?"
   "Who else should we talk to?"
   Thanks; confirm incentive.

7. Post-interview survey (optional)
   Any rated or multiple-choice items.
```

Include a **research-question map** at the bottom of every guide: which probes answer which research question. If a research question has no probe, the guide has a hole. If a probe answers no research question, cut it.

## The Mom Test check

Run every question against Fitzpatrick's three rules:

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Does the question mention our product, feature or concept? Then it invites them to be nice.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or opinions about the future.** Does it contain "usually," "would you," "do you ever," "how likely"? Rewrite toward a specific past instance.
3. **Talk less, listen more.** Is the question longer than one breath? Does it suggest an answer? Shorten it.

And watch for the three kinds of bad data he names:
- **Compliments** — "That sounds great." Worthless as evidence. Deflect and return to their life.
- **Fluff** — generics ("I always…"), future promises ("I'd definitely…"), hypotheticals ("I might…"). Anchor them to a real instance.
- **Ideas and feature requests** — useful, but dig under them: why do you want that, what would it let you do, how are you coping without it today?

## Best-customer / switch guide

Built on Laudi & Suellentrop's JTBD interview structure: life before, the trigger, the evaluation, onboarding, and the value they get now. Probes lean on Moesta's timeline and forces.

**Story prompt**
> "I'd love to go back to before you were using [product]. What was going on that made you start looking for something different?"

**Phase 1 — Before (the struggle)**
- What were you using to [job] at the time?
- What was working about it? What wasn't?
- Can you think of a specific time it let you down? What happened?
- Who else felt that? What did they say?

**Phase 2 — The trigger (first thought)**
- When did it go from annoying to "we need to change this"? What happened that day or week?
- Was there a deadline, a new person, a bad event, a growth spurt?

**Phase 3 — Looking and comparing**
- How did you start looking? Where did you go first?
- Who did you ask? (Note: peer and consultant recommendations are often the real channel.)
- What else did you consider — including keeping what you had?
- How did you narrow it down? What ruled options out?

**Phase 4 — Deciding**
- What made [product] the one?
- What almost stopped you? What worried you about switching?
- Who had to agree? Was anyone against it? What was their argument?
- What did you have to give up, migrate or relearn?

**Phase 5 — First use**
- What did you expect the first few weeks to look like? How did it actually go?
- When did you first feel it was working? What happened?

**Phase 6 — Value now**
- What can you do now that you couldn't before?
- If [product] disappeared tomorrow, what would you do?
- How would you describe [product] to a peer who'd never heard of it?
- What would you type into a search box to find something like it?

The last two are the highest-value questions for messaging: they harvest the customer's own category language.

## Win-loss guide

Built on Clozd's five principles: start broad and let the buyer tell their story; cover core topics in every interview; prepare drill-downs for recurring themes; keep questions open-ended (mostly *what* and *why*); adapt on the fly. Target 30 minutes.

**Story prompt (Clozd's broad openers)**
- What prompted you to evaluate [category] solutions at this time?
- How did you structure and run your evaluation?
- What did you decide, and why was that the right decision for your organization?

**Core topics — cover in every interview, in whatever order the story allows**
- *Requirements:* what the solution needed to do; which capabilities mattered most
- *Alternatives:* who else was considered; who won (on a loss) or was the strongest alternative (on a win); how clear our difference was
- *Product impressions:* strengths and weaknesses as they saw them
- *Sales experience:* what it was like; how it compared with other vendors; whether it affected the outcome
- *Pricing and packaging:* first reaction to price; comparison; whether the model fit; how they judged value
- *Buying committee:* who was involved and what role each played; whether everyone agreed
- *Implementation expectations:* what they expected the first 30–90 days to look like; concerns about getting up and running
- *Timeline:* what sped it up or slowed it down; whether a specific event triggered the final decision

**Drill-downs** — prepare one for each theme that recurs in your market. Clozd's example: never "Are integrations important?"; instead "Which integrations matter most to you, and why?"

**Rating items** go in a short follow-up survey, not the call.

## Churn guide

**Story prompt**
> "Take me back to when you first started thinking [product] might not be the right fit. What was happening?"

**Probes**
- What were you hoping [product] would do when you signed up? Did it?
- When did that change? Was there a specific moment?
- What did you try before deciding to leave? Did you contact us? What happened?
- Who made the call? Who else was involved?
- What are you using now — another tool, a spreadsheet, nothing?
- What would have had to be true for you to stay?
- Is there a situation where you'd come back?

Listen for the forces in reverse: the push away from you, the pull of the alternative (including "nothing"), and the anxiety and habit that kept them as long as they stayed.

## Non-switcher guide

**Story prompt**
> "You took a look at [category] options recently. Walk me through what got you looking and where you ended up."

**Probes**
- What made you start looking?
- What did you look at? How far did you get?
- What made you decide to stay with what you had?
- What would have had to be different for you to switch?
- What worried you about switching? What would you have had to give up or redo?

This is where anxiety and habit live. Expect answers like "we didn't have time to migrate," "the person who'd do it left," "what we have is fine for now."

## In the room

- **Open with them, not you.** Never start with a product overview. It turns the interview into a sales call.
- **Collect the story in order.** When they jump ahead, note it and come back: "Let's hold that — before that, what happened?"
- **Stay in the instance.** When they generalize, return: "In that particular case, what did you do?"
- **Ask for the artifacts.** The spreadsheet, the email to the board, the shortlist. Physical details anchor memory.
- **Use their words.** Repeat their nouns back. Don't introduce your category names.
- **Let silence work.** Wait three seconds after they stop. The second answer is often the real one.
- **Follow energy.** Torres: let the participant talk about what they care about most. Clozd: spend extra time where something was influential, skip what wasn't.
- **Never defend or correct.** If they're wrong about a feature, write it down — the misperception is the finding.
- **Don't take the pitch bait.** If they ask "can it do X?", say you'll follow up after, and ask why X matters.
- **Close with the two questions:** what should I have asked, and who else should I talk to.

## Recovery moves

| Situation | Move |
|---|---|
| One-word answers | Switch to a concrete prompt: "Can you walk me through the last time that happened?" |
| They want to vent about bugs | Let them for a minute, note it, then: "That's helpful. Going back to when you first chose us…" |
| They pitch features | "If you had that, what would it let you do that you can't now?" |
| They compliment the product | "Thanks. Tell me about a time it actually made a difference — what happened?" |
| They can't remember | Use memory cues: "What time of year was it? What else was going on at work?" Or accept it and move on — Torres: not every interview yields the story you want. |
| They don't have a relevant story | Ask about the nearest one they do have, then end early. Don't force it. |
| The interviewer leads | In debrief, mark the affected section as weak evidence on Rubric C. |

## Questions to retire

| Retire | Why | Replace with |
|---|---|---|
| "What features matter most to you?" | Research question asked aloud | "Tell me about the last time you used it for [job]." |
| "Would you pay $X for…?" | Hypothetical, future | "What did you pay for what you used before? How did you decide it was worth it?" |
| "Do you like our new design?" | About our idea; invites compliments | "Walk me through the last time you [did the task]." |
| "How likely are you to recommend us?" | A rating — belongs in a survey | (Move to post-interview survey) |
| "Why did you choose us?" | Invites a tidy, post-hoc reason | "Take me back to when you were deciding. What happened?" |
| "Don't you think it'd be easier if…?" | Leading | "How do you handle that today?" |
