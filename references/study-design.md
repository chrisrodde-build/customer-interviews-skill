## Start from the decision

Laudi and Suellentrop's argument for marketers is that research is only worth doing if it changes a growth decision — who to target, what to say, where the journey breaks. Torres makes the same point for product teams: the outcome you're working on sets the scope of discovery.

So write the decision first:

> We are deciding **[decision]**, and we'll know enough when **[evidence threshold]**.

Good: "We are deciding whether to fight for small accounts that churn in year one, and we'll know enough when we can name the two or three forces that drive those cancellations and whether any are within our control."

Bad: "We want to understand our customers better." It has no stopping rule and no owner.

Then write 3–5 research questions. They are what you need to learn, phrased however is useful to you. They will never be read to a participant.

## Choosing the interview type

| Type | Talk to | Best for | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Best-customer / switch | Recent, happy, high-value customers | Positioning, messaging, ICP, homepage, acquisition channels | Signed within the last ~3–12 months, so they remember choosing |
| Win-loss | Buyers who recently decided, either way | Competitive positioning, sales process, pricing perception | Within ~2 weeks of the decision (Clozd) |
| Churn | Recently cancelled or downgraded | Retention, onboarding, segment strategy | As soon after cancellation as possible |
| Non-switcher | Evaluated (trial, demo) and stayed with what they had | Anxiety and habit; why "no decision" wins | Within weeks of the abandoned evaluation |

Many studies need two types. For a messaging project, best-customer plus a few losses gives you both why people buy and what stops them. Run them as separate guides; don't blend.

## Who to interview

### Best-customer studies (Laudi & Suellentrop)

Interview the customers you want more of, not all customers. Their criteria, adapted:

- **Happily paying** — no discount fights, renewed or prepaid
- **Reached value without heavy hand-holding** — especially important for low-touch or product-led models
- **Urgent problem** — a painkiller, not a vitamin
- **Retained or expanded** — still here, ideally growing
- **Reachable and typical of a segment you can market to** — a unique unicorn teaches you nothing repeatable

Pull the list from data first (tenure, plan, usage, expansion, support load), then interview from it. Interviewing your best customers means results are a strong hypothesis about who else to attract, not a census of everyone.

### Win-loss (Clozd)

- The person who was **closest to the decision** — often not the most senior name in the CRM
- Contact within about **two weeks** of the decision, while the evaluation is fresh
- Mix wins and losses. Wins alone tell you why people who already agreed with you agreed with you.
- Where possible, more than one member of the buying committee on large deals

### Churn

- The person who **made or pushed** the cancellation, not just whoever owns billing
- Stratify by account size or age if churn reasons are expected to differ — they usually do

### Disqualifiers for any study

- Employees, friends, investors, active consultants or resellers (unless the study is about them)
- Anyone the interviewer sold to personally, for win-loss and churn
- People more than ~12 months from the decision you're studying — memory reconstructs

## How many

- **10–12 interviews per segment** is the practical saturation point Laudi and Suellentrop cite. You'll stop hearing new forces and start hearing repeats.
- Count **per segment**. Twelve interviews across four segments is three per segment — anecdotes, not patterns.
- Win-loss programs aim for a running sample across the quarter, not a one-off batch. A few per month beats a big batch once a year.
- If you have only 4–6, run them, and label the findings as directional.

## Survey first, or interviews first

Laudi and Suellentrop's rule of thumb: if you have roughly 500 or more customers who meet your best-customer criteria, a short qualitative survey first is efficient — it finds interviewees, sizes themes and pulls verbatims at scale. Below that, or if you can only do one method, do interviews.

Survey questions that work (open-ended, about the past):
- What was going on in your work that led you to look for something like [category]?
- What else did you consider, including keeping things as they were?
- What nearly stopped you from choosing [product]?
- What can you do now that you couldn't before?

End the survey with an opt-in for a 30-minute conversation and a few screener fields.

## Screener template

Keep it to 5–7 questions. It's a filter, not a survey.

```
1. Your role — [list; disqualify non-decision roles if needed]
2. Were you involved in choosing [category] at your organization? — Led it / Part of it / Not involved (disqualify)
3. When did you start using [product] / make the decision? — [ranges; disqualify outside window]
4. What were you using before? — [open text — this is gold even from people you don't interview]
5. Organization size / segment — [ranges matched to your segments]
6. Willing to do a 30-minute recorded video call? — Yes / No
7. Best email and time zone
```

Score responses with Rubric A (participant fit) and invite from the top down, filling each segment's quota.

## Invite templates

Principles: short, specific about time, clear it's research not sales, no pitch, give a reason they were chosen.

**Best-customer invite**
```
Subject: 30 minutes on how you chose [category]?

Hi [Name],

I'm [name], [role] at [company]. We're talking with a handful of
[customers like you / organizations like yours] about how they went
about choosing [category] — what was going on at the time, what you
looked at, and what's changed since.

It's a 30-minute recorded video call. No sales pitch, and nothing you
say will be attributed to you by name unless you say it can be.
As a thank-you, [incentive].

Would any of these work? [2–3 times] — or grab a slot here: [link]

Thanks,
[Name]
```

**Win-loss invite (send from a neutral person, not the rep)**
```
Subject: Your recent evaluation of [category]

Hi [Name],

Thanks for considering [company] for [category]. Whatever you decided,
we'd value 20–30 minutes to hear how your evaluation went —
what mattered, what didn't, and how the options compared.

I'm not in sales, and this won't turn into a pitch. [Incentive].

[Times / link]
```

**Churn invite**
```
Subject: Would you share what changed?

Hi [Name],

I saw that [organization] recently left [product]. I'm not writing to
win you back — I'd like to understand what changed, so we can do
better for others in your situation. Would you give me 20 minutes?
[Incentive].
```

## Incentives, scheduling, consent

- **Incentive:** match to the participant's world. Gift cards or account credit for customers; for nonprofits or public-sector buyers, a donation in their name is often more welcome than cash and avoids gift-policy problems. Win-loss buyers often participate without one; offer anyway.
- **Length:** 30 minutes for win-loss and churn (Clozd keeps win-loss to 30 minutes or less to avoid burdening buyers); 30–45 for best-customer.
- **Scheduling:** send a calendar invite with the video link and a one-line purpose. Remind the day before.
- **Consent:** state at the start that you're recording, why, who will see it, and how quotes will be used. Get a yes on the recording.
- **Who interviews:** someone trained and, for win-loss and churn, neutral. Two people is ideal — one asks, one notes — but the note-taker stays silent until the end.

## Common mistakes

- **No decision named.** The study wanders and the report changes nothing.
- **Sampling from the CRM's most responsive contacts.** You get champions.
- **One mixed study.** Wins, losses, churns and prospects in one guide, analyzed together. Different questions, different biases.
- **Too few per segment.** Four segments at three interviews each.
- **Waiting too long.** A loss from six months ago is a reconstructed story.
- **Pitching in the invite.** Participants arrive expecting a sales call and act like it.
